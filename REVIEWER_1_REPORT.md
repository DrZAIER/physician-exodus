## 1. Executive Summary

This manuscript presents a rigorous, empirically anchored, and highly policy-relevant dynamic cohort simulation and cost-consequence analysis of physician emigration from Tunisia across a 25-year horizon (2025–2050, spanning 2020–2050 simulation time). The authors successfully integrate the WHO Health Labour Market Framework (HLMF), discrete-choice random utility theory, continuous-time competing-risk demographic mechanics, and CHEERS 2022 economic evaluation standards.

From an LMIC health policy and systems perspective, this study is exceptional. It avoids the twin traps that frequently undermine health workforce research in middle-income countries: it does not retreat into unidentifiable, black-box agent-based models that alienate policymakers, nor does it rely on naive, unadjusted intention surveys that predict immediate health system collapse. 

Instead, the authors have built a **parsimonious, methodologically unassailable 36-cell cohort framework** that explicitly accounts for:
- The empirical **intention-to-action realization gap** ($\kappa = 0.35$).
- The mathematical **non-collapsibility of survey odds ratios** (via the Zhang & Yu 1998 transformation).
- The full **inframarginal public wage bill expansion costs** ($\Delta C = N_{\text{public}} \cdot \Delta w$).
- The **net human capital educational valuation** (incorporating a 40% resident clinical service offset).
- The critical **coastal vs. interior spatial equity divide**.

The primary finding—that expanding medical school quotas without fixing hospital working conditions functions as a **"Training-Only Trap"** that accelerates outward brain drain by 2,303 additional emigrants at a deadweight loss exceeding 117 Million TND—provides urgent, actionable intelligence for national decision-makers.

The manuscript is in an advanced state of readiness. I recommend **Publish with Minor Revisions**, focusing on contextualizing the policy levers within Tunisia's complex civil service, union, and fiscal governance landscape.

## 2. General Impression & Policy Value

For decades, Tunisia’s public health service (*Santé Publique*) served as a regional beacon of high-quality, state-subsidized medical education and universal primary care. However, the post-2011 macroeconomic slowdown, currency devaluation, and post-COVID fiscal constraints have triggered an unprecedented hemorrhage of medical personnel to France and Germany (~1,450 departures in 2024; >6,000 between 2021 and 2024).

The policy value of this manuscript lies in its **pragmatic fiscal and structural realism**:
1. **Dismantling the Single-Lever Myth**: The study conclusively proves that an across-the-board salary hike (SCEN_1: +30%) is economically inefficient ($\text{ICER}_{\text{public}} = 509,451\text{ TND}$ per public physician-year) due to the massive deadweight cost of paying the entire public physician workforce, while capital infrastructure upgrades (SCEN_2) achieve retention at less than half that cost ($\text{ICER}_{\text{public}} = 209,137\text{ TND}$).
2. **Quantifying the Macro-Fiscal Envelope**: The authors demonstrate that the Comprehensive Synergistic Reform package (SCEN_5) requires ~84.5 Million TND annually, representing **2.8% of the Ministry of Health’s annual operating budget**—a fiscally manageable target that can be presented directly to the Ministry of Finance (*Ministère des Finances*) and multilateral development partners.
3. **Actionable Spatial Intelligence**: By tracking the coastal-interior gap, the model demonstrates that isolated national retention leaves interior governorates with a density of only 4.8 per 10k by 2050, whereas a bundled regional package (SCEN_3/SCEN_5) elevates interior density to 5.4 per 10k (preserving 4,058 interior physician-years).

## 3. Major Health Systems & Implementation Comments

### 3.1. Policy Feasibility & Civil Service Remuneration Realities

The authors correctly model the full inframarginal public wage bill expansion ($\Delta C = N_{\text{public}} \cdot \Delta w$). However, to make this finding even more compelling to Tunisian policymakers, the Discussion should contextualize the institutional constraints of the **Tunisian Civil Service Statute (Law 83-112)** and public-sector union dynamics:
- **Cross-Sectoral Wage Parity & UGTT Dynamics**: In Tunisia, public-sector physicians (*Médecins de la Santé Publique*) belong to the civil service framework. Any substantial across-the-board base salary increase (+30%) negotiated with the medical syndicate (*Syndicat des Médecins, Pharmaciens et Chirurgiens-Dentistes de Santé Publique* / UGTT) inevitably triggers compensatory wage demands from other civil service corps (e.g., university professors, engineers, magistrates).
- **Non-Wage Compensation Feasibility**: In real-world budget negotiations, the Ministry of Health and Ministry of Finance frequently bypass civil service salary caps by expanding **non-base allowances**:
  - Re-evaluating on-call and night duty compensation (*indemnités de garde et d'astreinte*).
  - Introducing performance-based specialty bonuses (*primes de technicité et de rendement*).
  - Providing targeted tax exemptions on imported clinical equipment for returning diaspora doctors.
- *Recommendation*: The authors should explicitly note in Section 4.2 that non-wage fiscal allowances represent the most politically feasible vehicle for implementing the financial levers of SCEN_1 and SCEN_5 within civil service wage ceiling constraints.

---

### 3.2. Political Economy of Rural Retention & Return-of-Service (OTJM vs. Mandatory Laws)

Scenario 3 (Regional Retention Package) and Scenario 4 (Training Expansion with 75% Return-of-Service compliance, $R_{\text{ros}} = 0.75$) address the severe coastal-interior healthcare desertification. The implementation assumptions are well-supported by international evidence (Bärnighausen & Bloom, 2009; Frehywot et al., 2010), but benefit from North African political contextualization:
- **The Failure of Coercive Compulsory Service**: In Tunisia, past legislative attempts to enforce coercive mandatory rural practice (e.g., the contested 2014 draft health reform bill) triggered massive nationwide strikes led by the *Organisation Tunisienne des Jeunes Médecins* (OTJM) and medical residents, ultimately leading to legislative paralysis.
- **The Success of Incentive-Contractual Packages**: SCEN_3 correctly combines the +40% hardship allowance with subsidized residential housing and accelerated academic promotion. In Tunisian administrative practice, the single most powerful non-monetary incentive for young specialists is a **guaranteed right of transfer to a University Hospital Center (*CHU*) in Tunis, Sousse, Monastir, or Sfax after 3 years of interior service**.
- *Recommendation*: In Section 4.2 (Policy Recommendations), explicitly highlight that contractual return-of-service schemes succeed in Tunisia only when designed as **time-limited, incentivized contracts with guaranteed coastal/CHU transfer rights**, rather than indefinite compulsory placement.

---

### 3.3. The "Training-Only Trap": Inter-Ministerial Coordination (MESRS vs. MOH)

The model’s demonstration that expanding medical training intake by +15% (SCEN_4) results in **2,303 additional emigrants** and 117 Million TND in deadweight loss is one of the paper's most profound policy insights.
- **Inter-Ministerial Fragmentation**: In Tunisia, medical student admissions (*numerus clausus*) are determined by the Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research (*MESRS*), whereas hospital infrastructure, residency positions, and civil service recruitment are funded by the Ministry of Health (*MS*).
- **The Mechanism of Push Acceleration**: Expanding university enrollment without matching capital investment in university hospital (*CHU*) departments dilutes clinical training quality, increases patient-to-resident ratios, and worsens hospital incivility and burnout—thereby directly amplifying the push factors that propel graduates toward the French *EVC* and German *Approbation*.
- *Recommendation*: Emphasize in the Discussion that overcoming the "Training-Only Trap" requires institutionalized, binding joint governance between MESRS and MOH to link university intake directly to funded public residency and specialist posts.

---

### 3.4. Health Equity & Governance: Interior Desertification vs. Coastal Concentration

The 36-cell spatial modeling across Coastal/Metropolitan (Greater Tunis, Center East) and Interior/Underserved (North West, Center West, South) governorates provides clear, actionable equity metrics (@fig-spatial-equity):
- **Specialty-Specific Interior Collapse**: In interior regional hospitals (e.g., Kasserine, Sidi Bouzid, Jendouba, Tataouine), the shortage is not merely quantitative; it is specialized. The acute deficit is concentrated in **emergency medicine, anesthesiology, intensive care, obstetrics, and radiology**. When an interior hospital lacks an anesthesiologist, surgical and obstetric wards shut down completely, forcing dangerous medical evacuations to coastal CHUs.
- **Actionable Equity Metric**: The authors' estimation of $\text{ICER}_{\text{interior}} = 198,001\text{ TND}$ per interior physician-year retained under SCEN_3 provides a concrete benchmark for regional development funds (*Fonds de Développement Régional*).
- *Recommendation*: In the Results and Discussion, briefly highlight that the interior retention gain under SCEN_3 and SCEN_5 directly preserves critical acute-care lines (anesthesia and surgical coverage), preventing regional hospital service paralysis.

---

### 3.5. Multi-Stakeholder Migration Governance & European Destination Responsibility

The manuscript appropriately addresses bilateral responsibility under the *WHO Global Code of Practice on the International Recruitment of Health Personnel* (@who2021):
- **French and German Recruitment Pull**: France (via the PADHUE / *Loi Rist* hospital reforms and *Épreuves de Vérification des Connaissances* [EVC]) and Germany (via *Fachsprachprüfung* and *Approbation* recruitment drives) actively rely on Tunisian medical graduates to staff peripheral and rural public hospitals facing domestic shortages (DREES, 2024; BÄK, 2024).
- **Bilateral Financial Compensation & Circular Mobility**: High-income destination countries save approximately €200,000–€300,000 in direct training costs per recruited specialist. 
- *Recommendation*: In Section 4.2, strengthen the call for formal **Bilateral Health Workforce Agreements** between Tunisia, France, and Germany, featuring:
  1. Direct European co-financing for Tunisian CHU simulation centers and residency training infrastructure.
  2. Structured **circular migration / dual-practice visas**, allowing Tunisian specialists to complete 2-year advanced clinical fellowships in Europe with guaranteed reintegration into Tunisian university faculties.

## 4. Minor Conceptual & Contextual Clarifications

1. **Hospital Security and Drug Supply Chains**:
   In Section 4.2 (Item 2), when recommending capital investments in infrastructure (SCEN_2), explicitly mention:
   - Stabilizing the centralized pharmaceutical supply chain (*Pharmacie Centrale de Tunisie*, PCT) to prevent recurring stockouts of basic anesthetics, antibiotics, and oncology consumables.
   - Enforcing dedicated hospital security personnel and legal protection protocols in public emergency departments (*services des urgences*) to mitigate workplace violence against interns and residents (which Dhouib et al. 2024 identified as a top driver, $\text{OR} = 2.06$).

2. **Public-Private Interface (*Plein-Temps Aménagé*)**:
   In Tunisia, senior public hospital physicians are legally permitted limited private practice within public hospitals (*Activité Privée Complémentaire* / APC, or *Plein-Temps Aménagé*). The authors model a net public-to-private inter-sectoral transition hazard ($\lambda_{\text{trans}} = 0.018$). Mentioning this dual-practice nuance in the Methods or Discussion adds valuable institutional depth.

3. **Data Constraint Nuance**:
   The manuscript handles LMIC data constraints with exemplary rigor. The use of Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF), the Active Practice Ratio ($\alpha = 0.88$), and the Administrative Clearance Multiplier ($\lambda_{\text{exit}} = 0.75$) should be praised in the text as a gold-standard methodological blueprint for health workforce modeling in data-scarce settings.

## 5. Specific Recommendations for Strengthening Policy Impact

To maximize the translation of these findings into health policy action, I suggest the following minor textual enhancements:

1. **Include an Executive Policy Brief Box**: Consider adding a concise, 4-bullet "Policy Takeaways for the Ministry of Health and Ministry of Finance" summary box at the end of the Discussion, highlighting:
   - *Status Quo Cost*: Density falls to 7.7/10k; 23,767 doctors abroad by 2050.
   - *Ineffective Policy*: Training expansion without retention wastes 117M TND and exports 2,303 more doctors.
   - *Optimal Policy*: Synergistic Reform (SCEN_5) preserves 10,040 public physician-years at 2.8% of MOH annual budget.
   - *Regional Equity*: Regional incentive package preserves 4,058 interior physician-years, stabilizing interior density at 5.4/10k.
2. **Clarify Union and Civil Service Implementation**: In Section 4.2, briefly explain how the remuneration components can be operationalized through non-base allowances (*indemnités spécifiques*) to circumvent civil service wage freezes.
3. **Emphasize Circular Mobility**: Expand the bilateral recommendation with France/Germany to explicitly propose dual-affiliation fellowship models that convert "brain drain" into "brain circulation."

---

## 6. Final Recommendation

This is an exceptional, methodologically rigorous, and policy-actionable study that addresses one of the most critical health system challenges facing Tunisia and the wider North African region. The modeling choices are grounded in empirical reality, computationally parsimonious, and economically sound.

I recommend that the manuscript be accepted and **Published with Minor Revisions** following the incorporation of the contextual policy clarifications outlined above.
