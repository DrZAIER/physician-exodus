Dear Editor,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, **"Retention, return or training? A stock-and-flow simulation of Tunisia's physician workforce under incomplete migration data"**, for consideration as a Research article in *Human Resources for Health*.

**Why this manuscript fits the journal.** Tunisia is a high-emigration, middle-income country whose physician workforce debate is conducted with incompatible numbers: registered physicians (27 935 in 2022), practising physicians (15 315 in 2024) and reported annual departures (187 removal requests in the first half of 2023 versus 1 300–1 450 press-reported departures) are routinely conflated. The manuscript addresses a core HRH theme — health workforce mobility and migration — and does so in a way that is directly relevant to other middle-income countries with incomplete migration data.

**What we did.** We developed a transparent, reproducible, annual cohort-component model of physicians in active practice in Tunisia, calibrated to the national stock for 2017–2024. The model represents entry after postgraduate training, emigration before and after entry, return migration, retirement and mortality. We compared business as usual with five policy scenarios: remuneration and working conditions, career development, return migration, expanded training, and a combined retention and return strategy. We used 2 000 Monte Carlo draws, alternative effect sizes, and structural scenarios with rising emigration.

**What we found.** The observed stock path is nearly equally consistent with 675 to 1 839 annual emigrants: the emigration level is not identifiable from aggregate data. Within 15 years, retention and return policies outperform a 30% expansion of training, which is delayed by the training pipeline and partly offset by emigration of new graduates. A combined strategy adds about 7 350 physicians by 2040 and remains effective even if emigration intensity rises 3% per year.

**Why it matters.** The paper does not claim to predict the future or to estimate causal policy effects. Its contribution is to make the trade-offs explicit, to show which assumptions drive the conclusions, and to identify the data that would reduce uncertainty. It also offers a transferable methodological approach for countries where migration flows are poorly observed.
